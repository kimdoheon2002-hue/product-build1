Common Mistakes English Speakers Make When Learning Korean

Beginner guide · 5 min read

Korean and English have very different grammar structures, so many of the mistakes beginners make aren't random — they come from unconsciously applying English rules where they don't belong. Here are the ones that come up most often.

1. Word order: forgetting the verb goes last

English follows subject–verb–object order ("I eat rice"), while Korean follows subject–object–verb ("저는 밥을 먹어요" — literally "I rice eat"). Beginners often try to build Korean sentences in English order out of habit. The fix is simple but takes practice: the verb almost always comes at the very end of the sentence.

2. Skipping particles

Korean uses small grammar markers called particles (은/는, 이/가, 을/를, and others) attached to nouns to show their role in the sentence — subject, object, topic, and so on. English doesn't have a direct equivalent, so learners often drop them entirely. Without particles, a Korean sentence can become genuinely ambiguous, since word order alone doesn't always make the meaning clear.

3. Mixing up 안녕 and 안녕하세요

As covered in our greetings guide, 안녕 is casual and 안녕하세요 is polite. New learners often use them interchangeably because both translate to "hello" in English, but using 안녕 with someone older or unfamiliar can come across as disrespectful.

4. Ignoring speech levels (존댓말 vs. 반말)

This is one of the most common beginner mistakes, mostly because English has no grammatical equivalent. Learners memorize a verb in one form and use it everywhere, regardless of who they're speaking to. See our honorifics guide for a simple rule: default to polite speech until you're clearly invited to speak casually.

5. Reading Hangul letter-by-letter instead of by syllable block

Because Hangul groups letters into syllable blocks, sounding out a word letter-by-letter (like you might with the English alphabet) slows down reading and can distort pronunciation. Our pronunciation guide explains how to read each block as a single unit instead.

6. Relying only on romanization

Romanized Korean is a useful crutch at the very beginning, but it can't represent every sound accurately — especially the vowel and consonant distinctions covered in our pronunciation guide. Learners who rely on romanization long-term often develop pronunciation habits that are hard to unlearn later. Try reading Hangul directly as soon as you're comfortable with the basics, and use the audio buttons on this site to double-check your pronunciation against a native voice.

None of these mistakes are a big deal early on — they're a normal part of the process. The goal is just to notice the pattern so you can correct it sooner rather than later.
