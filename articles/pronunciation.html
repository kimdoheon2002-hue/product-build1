Korean Pronunciation Basics: Reading Hangul Aloud

Beginner guide · 6 min read

Hangul, the Korean writing system, is often praised as one of the most logical alphabets in the world — and once you understand how it's built, reading Korean out loud becomes much easier than it looks.

Hangul is built from blocks, not a straight line

Unlike English, which strings letters left to right, Korean groups letters into syllable blocks. Each block represents one syllable and is built from at least a consonant and a vowel, sometimes with a second consonant at the bottom.

한 = ㅎ (h) + ㅏ (a) + ㄴ (n) → "han"
One block, one syllable, read as a single sound.

This is why romanization (like "hangugeo" for 한국어) can look long — each Korean block usually becomes one syllable in the romanized spelling, and Korean words are often built from several blocks in a row.

Vowel sounds that don't exist in English

A few Korean vowels don't map cleanly onto English sounds, and they're worth practicing early:

  • ㅓ (eo) — an open "uh" sound, as in "law" but shorter. Different from ㅗ (o).
  • ㅡ (eu) — a flat, unrounded "eu" sound with no direct English equivalent — think of saying "uh" without rounding your lips.
  • ㅘ / ㅝ — combined vowels that glide from one sound into another, like "wa" and "wo."

Consonants with three intensity levels

Korean often has three versions of a similar consonant sound, distinguished by how much air and tension is used:

  • ㄱ / ㅋ / ㄲ — a soft "g," an aspirated "k" (with a puff of air), and a tense, sharp "kk."
  • ㄷ / ㅌ / ㄸ — soft "d," aspirated "t," and tense "tt."
  • ㅂ / ㅍ / ㅃ — soft "b," aspirated "p," and tense "pp."

These distinctions can genuinely change the meaning of a word, so it's worth training your ear on minimal pairs early on rather than treating them as interchangeable.

Use audio, not just romanization

Romanization is a helpful starting crutch, but it can't capture these finer sound distinctions accurately. That's why every phrase on this site includes a 🔊 button — tap it to hear the phrase spoken aloud and compare it to how you'd naturally read the romanized spelling.
